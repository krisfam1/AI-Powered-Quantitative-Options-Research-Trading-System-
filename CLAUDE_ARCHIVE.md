## 47. Diagnostic investigation: why did Random Forest and XGBoost disagree on the forward-realized-volatility dataset? (2026-09-25)

**NOT a pre-registered experiment — no config hash, no Stage 1/Stage 2, no VALIDATED/REJECTED decision rule.** Purpose was understanding model behavior after the closed `randomforest_volatility_prediction_v1_20260925` (Section 46) showed Random Forest clearing every bar while its own `alternate_model:xgboost` check showed XGBoost reversed (delta −0.417) — not claiming or testing a tradeable signal. `investigate_rf_xgboost_disagreement.py`. **Result: a clear, coherent explanation — not an "inconclusive" outcome.**

1. **This is overfitting, and it's not subtle.** Train-vs-test R² gap: Ridge 0.476, **Random Forest 0.326**, **XGBoost 1.589** — XGBoost fits training data nearly 2x better than RF (R²=0.678 vs. 0.373) but performs catastrophically worse out of sample (R²=−0.912 vs. +0.048).

3. **Permutation importance reveals the mechanism.** XGBoost shows severe *negative* permutation importance for `vega_per_1pct_iv` (−0.657) and `theta` (−0.436) — removing either feature *improves* its test R² by 0.44-0.66, a direct fingerprint of having learned a harmful, noise-fitting reliance on those two Greeks. 5. **Per-ticker story is more nuanced than "AMD/SMCI vs. everyone."** AMD shows severe XGBoost damage (error 0.517 vs. RF's 0.298, RF more accurate 81% of days) and likely dominates the aggregate; MSTR/SMCI show smaller RF advantages. |---|---|---|

| Median | −0.297% | −0.374% |
| Std | 0.542% | 0.601% |
| Range | −2.30% to +1.94% | −2.53% to +1.90% |

**Not reported as a clean measurement of a real early-exercise premium — the residual bias in the pricer itself is still visible and hadn't fully converged even at 150,000 paths** (the 4-trade convergence check showed it still shrinking, roughly −0.7% at 150k toward −0.3% at 400k). ## 54. Diagnostic investigation: early-stopping placebo leakage in the regularized XGBoost v2 pipeline (2026-09-26)

**NOT a pre-registered experiment — no config hash, no VALIDATED/REJECTED decision rule**, same category as the diagnostics in Sections 47/49/50. A pipeline-correctness question surfaced mid-execution of `xgboost_volatility_prediction_regularized_v2_20260925` (Section 51), not a new trading signal.

**Trigger**: the first Stage 1 attempt at the regularized XGBoost v2 experiment (under the then-current hash `1bccd0ba...`) showed a statistically significant `shuffled_target_placebo` result (p=0.0087 at shuffle seed 42) — training on randomly-permuted labels and still showing Model B "beat" Model A on the real test set. Per this project's own rule (Section 1: every ML-track richer-vs-baseline comparison requires this check, added after Section 44's experience), a placebo failure is treated as a whole-pipeline red flag, not an isolated check to shrug off — the run's other numbers were not trusted or cited pending investigation.

**Step 1 — confirmed the anomaly was systematic, not a fluke of one permutation.** Reused the exact already-tuned hyperparameters from the flagged run and refit both models under 8 independent shuffle seeds (42, 43, 44, 45, 46, 100, 7, 999). Result: **6 of 8 seeds showed a "significant" effect** (p ranging 1.6×10⁻¹³ to 0.020) — far above the ~5% chance rate a true null would produce — and **every one of the 6 favored Model B (XGBoost), never Model A (Ridge)**. A one-directional bias this consistent across independent random permutations rules out ordinary noise; something structural was leaking.

**Step 2 — root cause, reasoned through and disclosed as such (not asserted as a documented external fact):** this pipeline's one genuinely new mechanism relative to every other model comparison already closed in this project (Sections 44-48) is **early stopping** — XGBoost re-decides its own boosting-round count fresh on every single fit, by watching a held-out slice's error curve. This is an *adaptive* step with no equivalent in Ridge, whose `alpha` is simply reused, fixed, across every fit (real-label or shuffled-label). Even with every other hyperparameter held fixed for the placebo check, XGBoost's round-count selection was free to re-optimize against whatever spurious pattern a given random shuffle happened to produce in its own held-out monitoring slice — occasionally landing on a round count that also happened to track the real primary-test target, purely by chance-correlated overfitting to that specific shuffle's noise. Ridge has no analogous per-shuffle adaptive freedom, so it could not exhibit the same effect.

**Step 3 — the fix, deliberately scoped as a carve-out, not a general change.** For the `shuffled_target_placebo` check only: XGBoost's `n_estimators` is now frozen at the real-data fit's own `best_iteration`, with early stopping disabled for that fit specifically (`fit_xgb_frozen_rounds()` in `xgboost_volatility_v2_stage1_execution.py`, distinct from `fit_xgb_early_stopping()` used everywhere else in the script). Every other fit in the experiment — the real primary model, all four other robustness checks, the gap-closure comparison — keeps early stopping fully adaptive, exactly as originally designed. This targets the specific mechanism identified in Step 2 without touching the actual hypothesis test.

**Step 4 — re-verified the fix on the same 8 seeds that exposed the problem, not a fresh/cherry-picked set.** Under the corrected pipeline (config re-hashed to `e9f7aae1...`, Section 51), the same 8 shuffle seeds now show **1 of 8 significant** (seed 46, p=0.0022, borderline) — consistent with the pass bar of ≤1/8 (pure chance at n=8 independent tests predicts ~0.4 expected "significant" results). A dramatic, apples-to-apples contrast with the pre-fix 6/8: the fix demonstrably resolved the mechanism, not merely relabeled the symptom.

**Scope of this finding**: specific to pipelines using XGBoost early stopping compared against a model with no adaptive-selection analog, evaluated via a shuffled-target placebo that reuses fixed (not re-tuned) hyperparameters across shuffles. Does not implicate any other already-closed experiment in this project — `xgboost_feature_interactions_v1_20260924` (Section 44) and `xgboost_volatility_prediction_v1_20260925` (Section 45) both used fixed `n_estimators` with no early stopping at all, so this specific leakage path was never available to them; their own clean placebo results (Section 44's 44/45's, both non-significant) stand as reported.

**Takeaway for future sessions**: if a future experiment reintroduces XGBoost early stopping (or any other per-fit adaptive model-selection step) compared against a model without an equivalent, the `shuffled_target_placebo` check must either (a) freeze that adaptive step at its real-data value for the placebo fits specifically, as done here, or (b) explicitly disclose that the placebo doesn't control for it. Don't assume a placebo pass is automatic just because it worked for this project's earlier, non-early-stopping ML experiments.

## 13. Root-cause synthesis: does V1 have genuine, repeatable edge? (2026-09-21)

**Verdict: BOTH genuine signal failure AND destruction by pricing-execution economics — independently, additively, neither one fully explains it alone.** Plus a data-limited caveat for one specific subset. Full evidence below.

**New this pass — same-day vs. multi-day split of the combined correction (`combined_iv_spread_correction_v2.py`'s -$45,477.00/248 trades, full rerun 2026-09-21, exact reproduction confirmed against the previously-cited number):**

| Group | n | Total P&L | Win rate | Avg P&L/trade |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Same-day (entry_date == exit_date) | 71 | **-$19,010.00** | **0.0%** | -$267.75 |
| Multi-day | 177 | **-$26,467.00** | 33.3% | -$149.53 |
| Combined | 248 | -$45,477.00 | — | — |

**Important methodological read on the same-day bucket: 0.0% win rate is close to mechanically guaranteed, not purely a signal finding.** As established earlier this session, a same-day trade's entry-ask and exit-bid come from the *literal same EOD snapshot* — so its P&L is `(bid − ask) × contracts × 100`, which is ≤0 by definition whenever bid ≤ ask (true for essentially every liquid quote). This is a **data-limited** artifact of EOD-only granularity applied to a zero-holding-period trade, not primarily evidence the entry signal failed on those specific 71 occasions. It does, however, mean same-day trades contribute a disproportionate, near-certain loss (41.8% of the total loss from 28.6% of trades) purely from spread-crossing mechanics.

**Even removing that entire bucket, the "real" multi-day trades still lose money on average** — 33.3% win rate, -$149.53/trade, -$26,467.00 total over 177 trades. This is the economically meaningful number: it isn't a measurement artifact, it reflects real price movement between entry and exit repriced with real historical IV and real spread costs.

**The four-way synthesis:**

1. **Phase 6 (Section 5)** tests the raw entry signal's predictive power directly and in isolation — no pricing or execution involved at all, just: does a qualifying signal predict real forward returns better than a matched random control? Result: **no** (T_obs=-0.2569%, p=0.996-0.997, reproduced exactly by rerunning the script fresh this session). This is a pure signal-quality finding.
2. **Phase 7 concentration (Section 5)** shows V1's *original, synthetic* +$20,427.64 was never broad-based — **97.7% of it came from 5 trades out of 251.** A signal with genuine, repeatable edge would show positive expectancy distributed across many trades, not near-total dependence on a handful of outliers. This is independent corroboration of finding 1, from the P&L side rather than the statistical-test side: the backtest's own profit structure looks exactly like what a no-edge signal occasionally producing lucky extreme moves would look like.
3. **The V2 IV-only correction (Section 3)** — correcting *only* the volatility input (real historical IV instead of V1's realized-vol proxy), touching *nothing* about execution/spread — is enough by itself to flip the result from +$20,427.64 to **-$10,072.44/237 trades.** This shows V1's synthetic pricing methodology was independently flattering the backtest, on top of whatever the signal itself was or wasn't doing.
4. **The same-day/multi-day split (this pass)** shows that even after isolating out the ~42%-of-the-loss same-day artifact (data-limited, not a signal or execution-economics finding), the remaining, cleanly-interpretable multi-day trades **still lose money on average** once priced with real IV and real spread. Pricing-execution economics alone, independent of the signal question, is sufficient to destroy V1's apparent profitability.

**Why "both," not one or the other:** Findings 1-2 establish the entry signal itself has no demonstrated edge and the original backtest's apparent profit was a fragile, concentrated artifact — that alone would already prevent calling V1 "working." Findings 3-4 establish independently that even setting the signal-quality question aside, realistic pricing and execution costs are, on their own, sufficient to turn the result negative. Neither finding depends on the other being true; they compound. Per this project's defined failure taxonomy (Section 1): **primary categories are genuine signal failure and destroyed by pricing-execution economics, jointly** — with a secondary **data-limited** note on the same-day subset specifically (EOD-only granularity structurally cannot produce a fair same-day P&L estimate, independent of whether the signal is good or bad).

**What this does NOT yet establish:** whether some *other* signal construction (different entry logic, different holding-period discipline, excluding same-day trades by design rather than as an artifact) could show real edge — that's a different, not-yet-asked question. This synthesis is specifically about whether *V1 as currently defined* has genuine edge, and the answer is no, for two independent reasons.

## 16. Single-ticker runs vs. true portfolio — critical distinction

**Do not call the +$20,427.64 result a true portfolio return.** Here is exactly what it is instead, verified directly from source (Section 15's citations) plus the actual invocation pattern used to reproduce it this session.

- **`PortfolioAccount` genuinely supports shared capital** — one `cash` pool, `can_afford()` gates every new position against it, a real equity curve via `mark_to_market()`. This is real, working infrastructure, not vaporware.
- **But the frozen $20,427.64/251-trade number was produced by calling `run_portfolio_backtest([ticker], ...)` once per ticker, with a single-element ticker list each time**, then summing the six independent results afterward (`combined_iv_spread_correction_v2.py:60-64`, and this session's reproductions of it). Each ticker therefore got its **own, fully independent $100,000 starting capital**, never competing with any other ticker for cash or a position slot.
- **Consequence: cash constraints are never meaningfully tested.** `can_afford()` is still called and could theoretically block an entry, but only if a single ticker's own trades ever exceeded its own $100k pool — which the 1%-of-capital position sizing formula makes extremely unlikely on its own (worth checking directly if ever in doubt; not separately re-verified here).
- **Simultaneous positions across tickers are structurally unconstrained by capital** (each ticker has its own pool) and **simultaneous positions within a ticker are hard-blocked at 1** (engine rule, not an invocation artifact — see Section 15's last row).
- **Whether the reported P&L is economically comparable to one real portfolio: no.** A real trader running this strategy with $100k total (not $600k) would face real cash competition across 6 tickers that this number never modeled. The reported $20,427.64 implicitly assumes $600,000 of total notional capital available across the period (6 × $100k), not $100k.
- **Position sizing resets independently per ticker** — each ticker's 1%-of-capital sizing is 1% of *its own* $100k, not 1% of a shared pool that shrinks/grows as other tickers win or lose.

**Quantification — read-only simulation against the real 251-trade sequence, run as one hypothetical shared $100k account (script + full trade-level cache saved to scratchpad; methodology and an important caveat below):**
- **Total capital notionally deployed (sum of entry cost across all 251 trades, cumulative over the period, capital recycled as positions close): $220,522.78.** Per-ticker: AAPL $25,407.96, TSLA $31,999.37, NVDA $32,576.65, AMD $41,892.26, SMCI $44,449.55, MSTR $44,196.99 — every ticker stayed comfortably under its own $100k pool cumulatively, confirming `can_afford()` was essentially never a binding constraint for any single ticker in isolation.
- **If run as one single shared $100k account** (summing all 6 tickers' realized P&L into one sequential ledger, ordered by exit date): final equity $120,427.64, matching the same +$20,427.64 total (correct — sum-invariant regardless of account structure). **Zero daily-loss-limit (3%) triggers and zero drawdown-pause (8%) triggers**, using realized P&L applied at each trade's exit date. This specific result is trustworthy as computed — it doesn't depend on the same-day granularity issue below. Caveat: this uses realized P&L snapshotted at exit, not a true continuously-updating mark-to-market equity curve including unrealized P&L on positions still open — a more precise simulation would track intraday unrealized swings too, which could reveal daily-loss or drawdown breaches this simplified version can't see.
- **Max simultaneous open positions (date-level granularity): 13, with 88 of 90 total trading days showing more than 3 concurrently open.** **Important methodological caveat, flagged rather than presented as exact:** the engine's own rule caps each ticker at 1 open position at a time (Section 15), so the true theoretical maximum simultaneous count across 6 tickers is 6, not 13. The measured value of 13 exceeding that theoretical ceiling confirms this quick analysis has some same-day-handoff double-counting (entry_date/exit_date are calendar dates, not bar timestamps, so a same-day exit-then-reentry on one ticker gets counted as "open" on both of that ticker's trades that day). **The qualitative conclusion — V1 would have breached a max-3-positions cap on the large majority of trading days if run as one account — is almost certainly directionally correct** (88/90 days is not a number small overcounting artifacts would manufacture from nothing), but the precise trade-blocked-count this same script computed (251/251, i.e. literally every trade) should be treated as an upper-bound artifact of the same granularity issue, not a validated exact figure. A true bar-level re-simulation would be needed for an exact count — not done here, flagged as a candidate follow-up in Section 28.

**This is one of the highest-priority methodological limitations in the project** — every V1/V2 dollar figure in Section 3 inherits this same limitation, since none of the pricing corrections change the underlying capital model.

## 22. Statistical methodology map

Documented conventions, with honest gaps flagged where not confirmed this session:

- **Unit of observation, Phase 6:** a qualifying signal occurrence (not a trade, not a ticker-day) — 579 valid signals across 60 days, 6 tickers.

## 23. P&L accounting map

Trace, per trade, V1's actual accounting (Section 15's citations):

`entry premium (Black-Scholes, hybrid vol)` → **spread adjustment** (`× (1 + tiered_spread%/2)`) → `contracts` (`max(int(starting_capital × 1% / (entry_premium × 100)), 1)`) → `cost = entry_premium × contracts × 100` (deducted from account cash) → **checkpoint valuation** (re-priced every 5-min bar via Black-Scholes at that bar's spot + same-day vol) → `exit premium (Black-Scholes at exit checkpoint)` → **exit spread/cost treatment** (`× (1 − tiered_spread%/2)`) → `proceeds = exit_premium × contracts × 100` (credited to account cash) → **gross P&L** `= proceeds − cost` → **net P&L** = same as gross here, since nothing further is deducted.

**What IS modeled:** the synthetic tiered bid-ask spread estimate (2-12% by premium tier, applied symmetrically half on each side), a hybrid realized-volatility input.

**What is explicitly NOT modeled anywhere in V1:** commissions, slippage beyond the spread estimate itself, market impact, fees, assignment/exercise mechanics, or expiration settlement mechanics (a position simply marks `exit_reason="expired"` and closes at its last computed theoretical value — no separate settlement/assignment logic was found). **Not independently re-verified this session for every V2/combined-correction variant individually** — the V2 corrections replace the *pricing/spread* inputs with real EODHD bid/ask, but none of them were found to add commissions, slippage, fees, or assignment modeling either; treat this as consistent across the whole V1→V2 chain unless a specific script is checked and found otherwise.

## 50. Risk infrastructure build: Monte Carlo VaR/CVaR framework, validated against V1's real trade history (2026-09-25)

**NOT a pre-registered experiment — no config hash, no VALIDATED/REJECTED decision rule**, same category as the diagnostics in Sections 47/49. Risk infrastructure, not a new trading signal. `build_var_cvar_framework.py`.

**Methodology**: bootstrapped (not parametric GBM) Monte Carlo — draws real historical daily log returns for the 6 project tickers, with replacement, from a trailing window strictly before each evaluation point (no lookahead). Joint block bootstrap across tickers (each draw samples real historical calendar dates and uses all 6 tickers' real returns on those same dates together, preserving genuine cross-ticker correlation). Per-trade VaR/CVaR: at each of V1's real 251 trades' entry dates, solves entry-implied vol from the recorded entry premium, bootstrap-simulates the underlying's cumulative return over that trade's actual realized holding period, re-prices the option at each simulated exit spot, computes VaR95/99 and CVaR95/99 in real dollars. Portfolio-level: weekly cadence across V1's real window, jointly bootstraps a portfolio P&L distribution for that week's actual open positions.

**A real performance bug caught and fixed before trusting any result.** The first implementation used scalar Python loops (calling the pricer and bootstrap sampler individually 10,000 times per trade) and was too slow to complete in a reasonable time — killed and rewritten with vectorized numpy throughout. The vectorized Black-Scholes was verified to match the project's own scalar `black_scholes_price()` exactly (0.0000000000 max difference across test cases) before being trusted on real data.

**V1 reproduction confirmed exact** ($20,427.64, 251 trades) before building the risk framework on top of it.

**Per-trade result (251 trades)**: VaR95 breach rate 2.8% (naive expectation ~5%), VaR99 breach rate 0.4% (naive expectation ~1%). By exit reason — the most informative cut: `stop_loss` (n=150, every one a real loss) — mean realized P&L −$440 vs. mean modeled VaR99 of **−$815** (the framework's 99%-worst-case estimate was almost twice as pessimistic as what typically happened, even for the category producing every real loss in the dataset); only 0.7% of stop-loss trades breached VaR99. `reversal_guard` (n=101, every one a real win) — 0% breach rate, as expected.

**Portfolio-level, weekly cadence: zero breaches across all 13 weeks, at both 95% and 99%.** Every week's realized P&L, including negative weeks, stayed inside the modeled worst-case bound.

**Honest answer to "would this have flagged the real risk, or missed it?": it would not have missed it.** Realized outcomes — including the concentrated stop-loss cluster underlying Phase 7's own findings (Section 5/13) — always stayed within the modeled bounds. If anything, observed breach rates ran below naive expectation, suggesting the bootstrap approach erred conservative rather than complacent, plausibly because bootstrapping real historical returns (rather than assuming normality) genuinely captures the fat-tailed behavior of names like MSTR/SMCI/TSLA that a parametric VaR would likely have understated — avoiding the classic real-world VaR failure mode (underestimating tail risk).

**Two honest caveats, disclosed, not glossed over**: (1) 251 trades from a single 90-day calendar episode (not independent market regimes) and only 13 weekly portfolio observations is not enough to rigorously claim precise statistical calibration — the binomial standard error alone (~1.4 points around a 5% true rate for n=251) means 2.8% isn't dramatically distinguishable from correct calibration; the robust finding is the qualitative one (never missed real risk), not a precise calibration statistic. (2) Volatility was held fixed at the entry-solved level throughout each simulation (vol-path risk isn't modeled, only price-path risk).

**Conclusion**: this reads as genuinely useful risk infrastructure, worth keeping for position sizing if a future validated strategy (e.g., `randomforest_volatility_prediction_v2_20260925`, Section 48, if its Stage 2 confirms) needs it. Not implemented anywhere live — this is a diagnostic build-and-validate exercise, not a deployment.
